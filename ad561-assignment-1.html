PART 0 – Conceptual Warm‑Up

Write 2–4 sentences answering the following question:

“If a strategy makes money 70% of the time, is it automatically safe? Why or why not?” Use examples (real or hypothetical) to prove your point.

I would say that this depends on both the definition of 'making money', and the person employing the strategy. The answer I've landed on is no. An example that springs to mind is a person that charges a fee for their horseracing 'tips', I'm drawn to this as in my younger days back home I did subscribe to a service such as this. Betting 1000 dollars and winning back 1005 is still considered 'making money' but realistically not worth much to the gambler, especially one who's placing such high stakes! A person's greed will almost certainly ruin this strategy, lets say for the first 7 bets someone stakes 1000 dollars on each bet and each time nets a profit of 50 dollars. This leaves them with a balance of 1,350 if he/she is not compounding the winnings. For the last 3 bets they again stake their 1000 dollars however each time the bet loses. This leaves them with a balance of -1650. Unfortunately the only strategy that is automatically safe is one that makes money 100% of the time which currently I don't think exists, yet.

Interpretation Questions:

Did the shock change your final profit?

  • No, the rule was still implemented on the same day, because trading stopped immediately in both cases, the final cumulative P&L remained the same.

Did it change whether you survived?

  • No. The strategy still breached the –2,000 threshold on Day 53, so trading stopped at the same point. Therefore, the survival outcome did not change.

What mattered more: the rule or short‑run luck?

  • The survival rule mattered more than short-run luck. Although the price shock increased short-term losses, the stop-loss rule prevented further exposure and locked in the outcome. Once trading stopped, the further price movements had no impact on final equity.
